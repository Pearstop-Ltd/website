# Stephanie Wiechers: written voice profile

Source material: the UNSPSC guide written offline by Stephanie (unedited draft), plus the Dave Raywood / Exyte Hargreaves discovery call transcript (25 August 2026). This profile describes the written voice only. It sits alongside the spoken/podcast profile in `stephanie-voice-profile-v2.md`.

---

## 1. The shape of an argument

Stephanie does not open with a claim. She opens with the reader's situation, described accurately enough that the reader recognises themselves in it.

> "Many construction firms I speak face a similar challenge: they have a high-level idea of what they are purchasing, but are looking to improve decision making using their data."

The move is: situation, then the hidden problem inside the situation, then the reason the problem exists, then the method. The claim arrives last, if at all.

She frequently states the reason before the recommendation.

> "The reason is simple: construction projects are complex and all carry their own unique details."

**Rule:** never lead a section with a benefit. Lead with what the reader is currently doing and why it stops working.

## 2. Analogy from ordinary life

When she needs to establish an abstract principle, she stacks two or three everyday examples rather than explaining the principle directly.

> "If we invest, we will assess how we think the company or stock will perform prior. If we plan our route to the airport, or an important business meeting, we take possible delays into account."

The analogies are unglamorous and domestic. Airports, negotiations, stock picks. Never sports, war, or space.

**Rule:** one analogy stack per article, near the top, to earn the right to be abstract later.

## 3. Oddly specific examples

Her examples are too specific to be invented, which is why they land.

> "18 suppliers for windowsills, a project manager who always buys outside of framework contracts"

> "'gloves' as a description can be medical gloves, cleaning gloves, worker gloves or winter gloves"

> "hours will be subcontractor work but it can also be rental of machinery" (spoken)

> "actuators, dampers" (buyer's own words, adopted immediately)

**Rule:** every abstract point gets one concrete artefact attached. A real item, a real supplier behaviour, a real ambiguous description. Generic examples ("office supplies", "IT spend") are a failure.

## 4. Headings as buyer questions

Her section headings are the literal questions a procurement lead would type or ask.

- "What is UNSPSC and why would I use it?"
- "Who is responsible for correct labeling of procurement spend?"
- "How to assign UNSPSC categories when in doubt?"

**Rule:** headings are questions or plain statements of the task. Never noun phrases like "The classification imperative".

## 5. Name the painful process before the fix

She describes the failure state in detail, and only then offers the method. She does this even when it makes the current state sound worse than the sales narrative would prefer.

> "worst case every team member does their own research, further slowing down the process and creating inconsistencies across classification"

In the call, the same instinct appears as normalisation rather than judgement:

> "This is definitely codable. This is a very typical thing that we see."

**Rule:** describe the reader's current process without condescension. The reader should feel seen, not caught.

## 6. Mechanism over adjective

She never says "powerful", "cutting-edge", "seamless". She names the mechanism: guardrails, pipelines, rules, supplier weighting, confidence flag, human review, learning loop.

> "we've built specific AI pipelines with its own checks supplier databases that run the data through"

> "if there is an item which is hard to classify [...] that gets automatically flagged as low confidence. Then that pops up as a review. And then the great thing is that the system also learns from those things."

**Rule:** if a sentence contains a positive adjective about the product, replace it with the mechanism that produces the result.

## 7. Stating limits plainly

She admits what is not possible, immediately and without softening. This is the load-bearing credibility move in her writing.

> "it's impossible to predict months or even years ahead exactly what goods and services are required"

> "I have not. I've only reviewed it." (when asked if she had run his data)

> "I would have to check how many exactly."

**Rule:** where a limit exists, state it in a short sentence with no qualifier. Do not follow it with a recovery clause.

## 8. Sentence construction

- Short declarative sentences. Average around 15 to 20 words.
- Present tense for how things work. Conditional only for the reader's hypothetical.
- "We" for Pearstop, "you" for the reader, "teams" or "organizations" for the market.
- Lists are used for method steps and responsibilities, prose for reasoning. She does not bullet an argument.
- Numbered method steps written as "The first step is:", "The second step:". Direct, no scaffolding sentence before them.
- Parenthetical asides carry the specific example, not a caveat.

## 9. Register

Professional, matter-of-fact, CEO. She is the person who has already done the work, telling you what she found. Not a guide, not a coach, not an enthusiast.

There is a dry, understated humour available but she does not reach for it in writing.

> "We've tried and tested everything so you don't have to do it."

That is close to the ceiling of self-promotion in her written voice.

## 10. Hard constraints

Never use:

- Em dashes or en dashes. Use commas, colons, full stops.
- Exclamation marks.
- "quick one", "quick recap", "quick question", "hope you're well", "just checking in", "circling back", "reaching out", "no pressure either way", "happy to".
- Superlatives about Pearstop. No "leading", "best-in-class", "revolutionary".
- Volume metrics as proof of quality. Row counts and dataset sizes describe a problem, never demonstrate a result.
- Rhetorical questions used as transitions.
- Sentences that begin "In today's fast-moving..."

Always:

- One concrete artefact per abstract point.
- Reason before recommendation.
- Named mechanism instead of claimed benefit.
- Geography left out unless it is the subject.
- Tagline format for any summary line: {concrete result} {for who}. Number first, audience last.

## 11. Calibration passage

The following is written in profile. Use it as the target when checking a draft.

> Most teams moving to UNSPSC do not have anyone in-house who knows it. That is not a skills gap worth hiring for. It is a two-hour problem if you run it correctly. Put someone with real buying knowledge in a room with someone who knows the codes, work through the edge cases the business actually buys, and write down the decision each time. What takes months is the alternative: three buyers researching the same ambiguous line separately, each landing somewhere different, none of it written down.

Check a draft against this passage. If the draft is warmer, it is off. If it is more abstract, it is off.
